## Context

Iksula was a small company doing a big thing: helping premium Indian brands build their own e-commerce channels. In 2019–2021, brands like ITC, Eureka Forbes, and ELC Toys were watching Amazon eat their lunch and trying to figure out how to compete on their own websites.

My job started as "Business Analyst" but in practice looked a lot like "PM without the title." I owned the product lifecycle on multiple client accounts — from discovery to launch to performance optimization — with minimal oversight and a lot of accountability.

This is where I learned what product management actually is.

---

## The Problem

The brands coming to Iksula had a common complaint: *"Our website traffic is fine. Our conversions are terrible."*

The default response in the industry was to optimize the checkout flow — reduce friction, fewer form fields, faster payment. We tried that. The conversion needle barely moved.

When I mapped the full user journey for three client accounts, a different picture emerged:

- Users were arriving at homepages that showed the same content to everyone
- A customer who'd bought baby toys last month was seeing ITC's sauce promotions
- A returning Eureka Forbes customer was being shown a product category they'd never browsed
- Mobile sessions had checkout abandonment of 60%+ — but most of that abandonment happened at the product detail page, not the payment step

**The insight:** The problem wasn't checkout. The problem was discovery. Users were leaving because they couldn't find what they wanted, not because paying was too hard.

---

## My Role

I was the product lead on three accounts simultaneously:
- Defined product requirements and wireframes
- Managed the technology partners building the underlying platform
- Ran A/B tests and analyzed performance data post-launch
- Presented recommendations directly to client leadership
- Owned go-to-market coordination with each brand's marketing team

I was 23. I was figuring this out as I went.

---

## Process

### Discovery

I ran user session recordings (Hotjar) and behavioral analysis (Google Analytics) across all three client accounts for 4 weeks. What I was looking for: where did users hesitate? Where did they scroll back up? Where did they leave?

The patterns:
1. **Homepage personalization gap** — returning users saw the same hero banner as new users. There was no personalization layer at all.
2. **Category navigation complexity** — brands like ITC had 15+ product lines. The nav was organized by internal business unit logic, not customer mental models.
3. **Mobile product detail pages** — key product information was buried. On mobile, users had to scroll 3-4 screens to find price and availability.

### The Personalization Bet

The personalization engine was the riskiest call I made in this role. It was:
- **More expensive** to build than a checkout optimization
- **Harder to measure** — you need time and volume to see the impact
- **Invisible to the client** initially — they couldn't see it working in a demo the way a new homepage design was visible

I made the case to ITC's digital team using a competitive analysis: I showed them their Amazon.in performance (where their products *did* convert well) vs. their own site. The difference was almost entirely personalization — Amazon knew what each user wanted; ITC's site did not.

They approved it.

### Execution

The personalization engine used browsing history and purchase data to serve:
- Personalized hero banners for returning users (category-specific)
- "Recently viewed" and "You might also like" modules on the homepage
- Category-specific email triggers for cart abandonment

In parallel, I worked on mobile UX improvements — restructuring product detail pages to surface price, availability, and the buy button above the fold on mobile.

### Iteration

Post-launch, I ran A/B tests for 8 weeks. The data showed:

- Personalized homepage → +8pp conversion uplift vs. control
- Mobile PDP restructure → +6pp on mobile specifically
- Abandoned cart emails (personalized) → 22% recovery rate

Combined effect across accounts: **+16 percentage points** on overall conversion, anchored by ITC as the lead account.

---

## Key Decisions

| Decision | What I chose | Why | Alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discovery prioritization | User journey analysis before any solution | Conversion problem wasn't where everyone assumed | Jump to checkout optimization (industry default) |
| Personalization vs. checkout | Personalization first | Data showed discovery was the gap; checkout was fine | Checkout optimization — less risky, faster to build |
| A/B test duration | 8 weeks | Enough traffic to hit statistical significance across all 3 accounts | 2-week test — faster, but would have produced false positives |
| Client communication | Weekly written updates with data | Builds trust, surfaces problems early | Monthly review — slower feedback loop |

## Outcome

By the end of the engagement:

- Conversion rates improved by **+16 percentage points** across client accounts (from a low baseline — this was a meaningful absolute change)
- All 6 products shipped on time, within scope
- **95% client satisfaction** score across accounts (measured via Iksula's internal client health reviews)
- Received **Quarterly Excellence Award** — the first time it had been given for "product ownership mindset" in a BA role

Most importantly: this is where I developed the habit of leading with data, not instinct. Every recommendation I make in product conversations today, I trace back to learning it the hard way at Iksula — where the numbers were the only thing that mattered and clients noticed immediately if you were guessing.

---

## What I Learned

**1. The presenting problem is rarely the actual problem.**
Every client said "checkout is broken." Checkout was fine. Discovery was broken. I learned to be skeptical of the first diagnosis and to look at the full journey before picking a solution.

**2. Risky bets need a strong narrative, not just data.**
The personalization engine was the right call — but it wasn't obvious. I had to make an argument: here's what Amazon does, here's what you do, here's the gap. Data gives you the case; narrative closes it.

**3. Product ownership is not about authority — it's about accountability.**
I had no formal authority over the technology partners building these products. I couldn't fire anyone or stop a sprint. What I had was clarity about the goal and willingness to be accountable for the outcome. That turned out to be enough.

**4. Ship fast, measure everything, change your mind when the data says to.**
In 2 years I shipped 6 products. Not all of them worked perfectly the first time. The ones that succeeded are the ones where we measured closely and iterated quickly. The ability to change your mind based on evidence is a product superpower.
